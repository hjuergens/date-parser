The language

Small enough for every tool, rich enough to show their differences: three date notations, periods, business days, IMM and FX spot dates, lists and ranges. A schedule is a rule: a function of the base date T and the market.

schedule = range | item { "," item } .
item     = expr [ "IF" WEEKDAY { WEEKDAY } ] .
range    = "(" expr ":" period ":" expr ")" .
expr     = date { ("+" | "-") period
               | ">" ( "IMM" | "SPOT" [ PAIR ] | ROLL
                       | ("NEXT" | "PREV") anchor )
               | "{" shift { "," shift } "}" } .
shift    = WEEKDAY ("+" | "-") period .
anchor   = WEEKDAY | "BD" | "IMM" | annual | EVENT .
date     = "T" | "TOM" | "SPOT" [ PAIR ]
         | NUM ( "-" NUM "-" NUM       (* ISO    *)
               | "." NUM "." NUM       (* German *)
               | "/" NUM "/" NUM )     (* US     *)
         | annual | EVENT .
annual   = NUM MONTH                   (* 4 JUL     *)
         | NUM WEEKDAY MONTH           (* 3 MON JAN *)
         | "LAST" WEEKDAY MONTH | "EASTER" .
period   = NUM ( "D" | "W" | "M"
               | "Q" | "Y" | "B" ) .
ROLL     = "FOLLOWING" | "PRECEDING" | "MODFOLLOWING"
         | "BOM" | "EOM" | "BOY" | "EOY" .
MONTH    = "JAN" | … | "DEC"           (* Gregorian *)
         | "MUHARRAM" | … | "DHULHIJJAH"  (* Hijri *)
         | "NISAN" | … | "ADARII"     (* Hebrew  *)
         | "ZHENGYUE" | … | "LAYUE" . (* Chinese *)
EVENT    = "@" UPPER { UPPER | DIGIT | "_" } .  (* @FOMC *)

In grammatical terms (after grammatical category): the upper-case symbols are lexical categories, word classes that the lexer recognises; the lower-case rules are phrasal categories that the parser builds from words. The numbers carry the content, and the grammar adds a few things with a small, closed set of forms: number (one date, or several from a list or range), tense (T is the base date, the anchor date that tense relates to; TOM and SPOT are relative to it, > NEXT and > PREV look forward and back) and definiteness (4 JUL is the one in the year of T, > NEXT 4 JUL the next one).

Every decision is LL(1): after the first number, the separator selects the notation; after a complete date, + or - starts a period. Periods follow java.time: months first, with the day clamped to the end of the month (2016-01-31 + 1M is 2016-02-29), then days. B counts business days, Monday to Friday. > IMM moves to the next IMM date strictly after the date: the third Wednesday of March, June, September or December. > SPOT moves to the FX spot date: + 1B for USD against CAD, TRY, PHP or RUB, + 2B for every other pair (PAIR is six capital letters such as EURUSD).

Parsing a schedule yields a rule, not dates: a function that gets the base date T and a market only when it is evaluated. The grammar never names a market. A market only answers questions about days (which days are business days, and so which day is spot); the meaning of a rule doesn't depend on it, so IMM is the third Wednesday everywhere. Without a market, only Saturdays and Sundays are skipped.

Holiday rules: 4 JUL, 3 MON JAN, LAST MON MAY and EASTER are dates in the year of T. An observance {SAT - 1D, SUN + 1D} shifts a date by the first item for its weekday, so 4 JUL {SAT - 1D, SUN + 1D} is Independence Day as the NYSE observes it. A market can be declared from such rules; the test markets in the case list below are. Rolling, seen from T: TOM and SPOT are dates; > NEXT FRI, > PREV IMM or > NEXT 4 JUL look strictly after or before the date; > EOM and friends move to the edge of month or year, and > FOLLOWING, > PRECEDING and > MODFOLLOWING to a business day of the market. Hijri (Umm al-Qura), Hebrew and Chinese months are month names too: 1 SHAWWAL is the day in the year of T, an error if it falls there twice or not at all. 24 DEC IF MON TUE WED THU keeps a date only on these weekdays.

What the comparison shows

  • Top-down needs right recursion, bottom-up likes left recursion.

    The LL tools and Peggy write date { ("+" | "-") period } as a loop. CUP and Bison write expr '+' period, which is natural for LALR(1).

  • Separating syntax from semantics is a choice.

    SableCC's grammar contains no code at all; a separate tree walker computes the dates. Every other tool interleaves actions with the rules.

  • Compute while parsing, or build a rule first.

    Because the market only arrives at evaluation, no tool can compute the dates while parsing. The action-based tools (ANTLR, Coco/R, JavaCC, CUP, Bison, Peggy) now build functions in their actions; in SableCC the syntax tree already is the rule, and the visitor, transformer and combinator versions only gained a parameter. A side effect: Coco/R's actions no longer compute anything during error recovery.

  • Error recovery has consequences.

    Coco/R recovers and keeps executing actions on incomplete input, so the actions must tolerate it. ANTLR needs a bail strategy to stop at the first error.

  • Ordered choice is not alternation.

    PEG tries the alternatives in order and commits to the first match. Here this is harmless because the separators differ, but it's the main semantic difference to the generated LL and LR parsers.

  • The library matters as much as the parser.

    In JavaScript, about 35 lines (plus 25 for markets, business days, IMM and spot dates) reproduce java.time's month arithmetic. On the JVM, Period can't express business days, so B needs a TemporalAmount of its own. The end-of-month cases in the test file exist to catch differences.

  • One grammar, two algorithms.

    Lark builds an Earley parser or an LALR(1) parser from the same grammar file; every shared case runs with both. Earley accepts any context-free grammar, including ambiguous ones, but this language doesn't need that.

  • Actions tie a grammar to one language.

    ANTLR's Java grammar computes the dates in Java actions, so it can't be generated into Python. The Python module uses the same rules without actions and a visitor, which would work for every ANTLR target.

  • A lexer may or may not know the parser's state.

    >SPOTUSDCAD without a space: ANTLR, Coco/R, JavaCC, JFlex and SableCC read SPOTUS as a currency pair and fail. Peggy and the Scala combinators have no lexer, and Lark's contextual lexer only tries the tokens the parser expects; all three accept it.

  • Six capitals: keyword or currency pair?

    EASTER has six capital letters, like EURUSD. The tools with a lexer decide by order: ANTLR's implicit keywords come first, JFlex, JavaCC and SableCC take the rule declared first, Coco/R checks its keyword table, and Bison's hand-written scanner tests the keywords before the length. Without a lexer, or with a contextual one, the pattern itself must exclude the keyword (!"EASTER" in Peggy, (?!EASTER) in Scala and Lark), otherwise T > SPOT EASTER would read a pair. Lunar month names such as TISHRI joined the list. Event names avoid the race altogether: the sigil in @EASTER makes it a name in every tool.

  • Several dates from one expression, without touching the expression rules.

    @FOMC + 1B has a date per FOMC decision, while every grammar builds an expression as a function returning one date. Instead of changing that in ten places, the event's date throws until it is bound: the list catches it, looks up the event's dates in the year of T and evaluates the item once per date. The grammars only gained the token and two alternatives.

  • A shared test file keeps everyone honest.

    Every module reads examples/schedule.txt. Several tool-specific pitfalls, listed in the tabs above, surfaced only because the same cases had to pass everywhere.

  • Generated cases test the libraries too.

    2186 more cases come from a holiday rule catalog and from finance-date research, with the dates of that project's own engine. They showed that ICU's astronomical Chinese calendar, in ICU4J and in Node's Intl, puts Chinese New Year 2027 and 2030 a day off: the new moon falls within minutes of midnight in Beijing. The JVM modules and Peggy now take Chinese dates from 6tail's lunar library. 3302 further cases translate the rules of date-holidays (data CC BY 3.0) for 207 countries and their regions, with that library's own dates; where its Hijri or Hebrew calendar disagrees with Umm al-Qura or the Hebrew calendar, the year is left out and counted.

Run it yourself

You need JDK 17 and Maven 3.9. Bison is optional: without it the Bison module builds empty. The Peggy module downloads its own Node.js.

git clone https://github.com/hjuergens/date-parser.git
cd date-parser
mvn clean verify                      # all modules
mvn -pl date-rule-coco test           # one module
